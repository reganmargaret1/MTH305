    Background
    🔗
    Cancer is approaching heart disease as the leading cause of death in the United States [1]. Knowledge of tumor growth is important in cancer screening planning and treatment. Effective models of tumor growth not only help researchers understand the dynamics of cancer growth and evaluate screening strategies, but they also enable clinicians to predict the efficacy of cancer therapies and optimize treatment protocols.
    🔗
    In this project, you will investigate several classical models for cancer growth and examine the behaviors of these equations and their solutions through qualitative techniques. Furthermore, you will evaluate how the predictions of tumor growth change when chemotherapy or other treatments are introduced, such as, how does the maximum tumor size change? What is the minimum effective concentration of the drug needed? Analysis and data fitting may be carried out to assess the models’ ability to predict future growth of tumors and shed some light on the development of resistance to cancer treatment.

    Part 1: Modeling Scenarios
    🔗
    The growth of cancer can be modeled through first-order ordinary differential equations by examining the change of tumor volume \(V\) over time \(t\text{,}\) given an initial volume \({V(0) = V_{0}}\text{.}\) In this first section, we will go through some models historically used to model tumor growth. The initial condition \({V(0)=V_{0}}\) is used in the following models, and the parameters in the models are assumed to be positive. Make sure you answer each of the questions for each of the 5 model types.
    🔗

    1.

    A simple exponential model describes the early stages of tumor growth, where the growth is proportional to the tumor population. Cells divide and create two daughter cells at a constant rate that is independent of tumor size. The model is given by
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dV}{dt}= aV \end{equation*}
    This model was first applied to cancer growth in 1956 [2]. The tumor doubling time, that is the time it takes for the tumor to double in size, was additionally introduced to measure how fast a tumor grows and quantify the growth rate,
    \begin{equation*} DT = \frac{\ln 2}{a} \end{equation*}
    The exponential model predicts early growth well. However, exponential growth is not valid for the long-term growth of solid tumors. Thus we investigate several alternative models below.

    3.

    The Bertalanffy model (after C. Von Bertalanffy) balances synthesis and destruction [3]. It assume that tumor growth is proportional to surface area, since nutrient enters through the surface, and that death is proportional the tumor size. Thus we arrive at the model given below, also known as the surface rule model:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dV}{dt}= aV^{2/3}- bV \end{equation*}
    This model has been successfully applied to describe human tumor growth. The exact solution of the Von Bertalanffy differential equation model is given below.
    \begin{equation*} V(t) = \left[\frac{a}{b}+\left(V_{0}^{1/3}- \frac{a}{b}\right)e^{-bt/3}\right]^{3}. \end{equation*}

    4.

    The logistic model of population growth was first published by Pierre Verhulst in 1845 [11]. The model assumes a linear decrease of the relative growth rate with population size. The maximum size is limited by a carrying capacity \(\frac{a}{b}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dV}{dt}= aV\left(1-\frac{V}{a/b}\right) = aV\left(1-\frac{b}{a}V\right). \end{equation*}

    5.

    The Gompertz model was created by Benjamin Gompertz in 1825 to explain human mortality curves and determine the value of life insurances [5]. It was applied to organism growth a hundred years later. The Gompertz model exhibits an exponential decay of the growth rate. It has been successfully used to model breast and lung cancer growth. If we let \(r(t)\) be the growth rate that exhibits exponential decay, then we have a system of differential equations given by:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dV}{dt}= r(t)V(t), \quad \quad \frac{dr}{dt}= -br(t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    However, from the above equations we can rearrange and find that:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dt}\left(\ln V\right) = \frac{1}{V}\frac{dV}{dt}= r(t) = -\frac{1}{b}\frac{dr}{dt}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus,
    \begin{equation*} \ln(V) = \frac{-r(t)+a}{b} \end{equation*}
    for some constant \(a\text{.}\) Solving for \(r(t)\) leads to \(r(t) = a-b\ln V\text{.}\) Substituting this into the system of two differential equations above, we obtain an alternative and more popular form of the Gompertz model:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dV}{dt}= V(a-b\ln V). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Solve the differential equation for tumor volume \(V(t)\text{.}\) Do not assume specific values for \(a\) or \(b\text{.}\) (You can solve this using technology, or previous knowledge.)

    Part 2: Model Fitting and Analysis
    🔗
    In the second part of this project, you will fit models to real cancer data to predict the best fit model for tumor growth, Gompertz, Logistic, or Bertalanffy. The fitting is performed in python by minimizing the residuals, that is the difference between the real data value and what the model predicts the data value should be.
    🔗
    To measure the “goodness or fit”, we will want to use the normalized mean square error (NMSE):
    \begin{equation*} NMSE = \frac{\sum\limits_{i} (y_{i} - \hat y_{i})^{2}}{\sum\limits_{i} y_{i}^{2}}. \end{equation*}
    The values \(y_{i}\) represent the real data values, the values \(\hat y_{i}\) represent what your model thinks (predicts) the data values should be. The NMSE is independent of measurement units and scale. A value of NMSE close to zero indicates a good fit of the model. You will need to download and clean the data carefully, before you can use it in your analysis.
